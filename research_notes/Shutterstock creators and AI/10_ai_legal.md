# Legal and copyright developments: AI-generated stock imagery and creator income

**Scope/currentness:** United States and EU developments checked for this note through September 2026. This is a research summary, not legal advice. Rules differ by jurisdiction; many training-data disputes remain unresolved.

## What key US and EU rulings or policies change for image creators?

### Takeaway
US policy recognizes copyright in human-authored contributions, not purely machine-generated material; the unresolved training-use question remains fact-specific. The EU has a more explicit text-and-data-mining (TDM) framework and AI-transparency duties, but neither the DSM Directive nor AI Act creates an automatic royalty for each creator whose work was used.

### Cited Findings
- The US Copyright Office says AI outputs are copyrightable only where a human determined sufficient expressive elements; prompting alone generally does not establish authorship, while human selection, arrangement, or modification may qualify. It rejects a categorical rule and assesses the human contribution case by case. [US Copyright Office, Part 2 report (2025)](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf)
- In *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, the D.C. Circuit affirmed that a work generated autonomously by AI without human authorship cannot be registered under the Copyright Act. This establishes the human-authorship threshold, not the status of every AI-assisted image. [D.C. Circuit opinion, 2025](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/docs/Thaler-v-Perlmutter.pdf)
- The Copyright Office’s May 2025 Part 3 report concludes that some AI training uses may be fair use, while others may not; it emphasizes purpose, access/market effects, outputs and licensing-market development. It does not settle legality across models or datasets, and the report is explicitly a pre-publication version pending final publication. [USCO Part 3 (pre-publication, 2025)](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf)
- EU DSM Directive Article 4 permits commercial TDM of lawfully accessible works unless the rightsholder has expressly reserved rights (for online content, in an appropriate machine-readable manner). Article 3 creates a distinct exception for qualifying research organizations and cultural heritage institutions. National implementation and the facts of access/reservation matter. [Directive (EU) 2019/790, arts. 3–4](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2019/790/oj)
- The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) requires general-purpose AI model providers to maintain a policy for complying with EU copyright law, including respecting Article 4(3) rights reservations, and to publish a sufficiently detailed summary of training content. These are provider transparency/compliance duties, not an individual compensation mechanism. [AI Act, arts. 53(1)(c)–(d), 113](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)
- As of the EU Act’s current phase-in, the Act generally applies from 2 August 2026, while certain obligations have different dates and transition rules. Check the consolidated text for implementation timing and any subsequent amendments. [AI Act, art. 113 and EUR-Lex current text](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)

### Inferences
- EU creators may have a statutory opt-out pathway for commercial TDM; effective reservation, proof of rights ownership, and cross-border enforcement are practical dependencies.
- US creators cannot assume that unlicensed training is either categorically lawful or categorically infringing. The legal result can turn on particular works, dataset acquisition, model behavior, and market evidence.

### Gaps
- No final US Supreme Court ruling establishes a general fair-use rule for generative-model training as of this note.
- EU national court rulings interpreting Article 4’s reservation mechanism for generative-image training remain unsettled/fragmented; no uniform royalty entitlement was identified.

## What do training-data claims and licensing developments mean for creator income?

### Takeaway
Copyright lawsuits increasingly test whether copying works for training and whether generated outputs reproduce protected expression, but complaints are allegations and interlocutory decisions are narrow. Licensing and platform terms are currently clearer mechanisms for payment than a general judicially established training royalty.

### Cited Findings
- In *Andersen v. Stability AI*, artists allege copyright and related claims involving training and generated images. The live docket shows continuing proceedings in 2026; allegations should not be represented as findings of liability. [CourtListener docket (PACER/RECAP)](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/66732129/andersen-v-stability-ai-ltd/)
- In *Bartz v. Anthropic*, the Northern District of California held that training on lawfully acquired books was fair use on the record before it, but acquisition/retention of pirated books was not thereby immunized; the court ordered a separate trial on alleged pirated-copy damages. This was a books/text case and does not automatically decide image-training claims. [Order, *Bartz v. Anthropic*, No. 3:24-cv-05417 (N.D. Cal. June 23, 2025)](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/69056867/bartz-v-anthropic-pbc/); [Reuters coverage](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/anthropic-wins-key-us-copyright-ruling-ai-training-case-2025-06-24/)
- *Kadrey v. Meta* granted Meta summary judgment on the plaintiffs’ specific training claim, finding the evidence insufficient to establish market dilution in that case; the court cautioned the ruling was not a blanket rule that AI training is fair use. The decision is fact-specific. [Order, *Kadrey v. Meta Platforms*, No. 3:23-cv-03417 (N.D. Cal. June 25, 2025)](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/67583792/kadrey-v-meta-platforms-inc/); [Reuters coverage](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/meta-wins-ai-copyright-lawsuit-authors-2025-06-25/)
- Getty Images’ US case against Stability AI was dismissed in 2025 on jurisdictional grounds as to the principal US copyright claims, without a merits determination whether training infringed; the UK case proceeded on narrower issues. [*Getty Images (US), Inc. v. Stability AI, Inc.*, No. 1:23-cv-00135 (D. Del.) docket](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/66725573/getty-images-us-inc-v-stability-ai-inc/); [Reuters, UK ruling report](https://www.reuters.com/world/uk/uk-judge-rules-against-getty-images-most-copyright-claims-against-stability-ai-2025-11-04/)
- Shutterstock has a Contributor Fund intended to share licensing revenue from certain AI partnerships with contributors whose content is used in training, subject to the fund’s methodology and eligibility. This is platform/program compensation, not a general statutory right to payment. [Shutterstock, AI and Contributor Fund information](https://www.shutterstock.com/blog/ai-generated-content-licensing-contributor-fund)
- Shutterstock’s contributor policy disallows submitting AI-generated content to its regular content library; this is a contractual/platform acceptance rule and should not be confused with copyright law. Always review the current contributor agreement and fund terms. [Shutterstock Contributor Support](https://www.shutterstock.com/contributorsupport/en/articles/10594621-AI-Generated-Content-Policy)
- Shutterstock’s terms and contributor materials define contractual grants and payment conditions. Whether a contributor authorized training, what works are covered, and how an AI fund allocates compensation depend on the applicable agreement/version and program rules. [Shutterstock contributor legal terms](https://submit.shutterstock.com/legal/terms?language=en)

### Inferences
- Near-term creator income exposure can arise through competition/substitution for stock-image demand even where a training-use claim fails; copyright doctrine does not guarantee compensation for economic displacement alone.
- Creators should distinguish (1) licensing a work to a platform or model developer, (2) platform-funded distributions, and (3) statutory/remedy claims in court; each has different eligibility, attribution and audit terms.

### Gaps
- Public-facing fund summaries do not fully disclose each platform’s complete allocation formula, contributor-level audit rights, or whether all historical portfolio images are included; consult current program terms/account statements.
- Cases are active and appellate developments may alter the status of trial-court outcomes. Docket snapshots are not substitutes for reading operative orders.

## What identifiable legal and commercial risks should stock creators watch?

### Takeaway
The most concrete risks are loss of control through broad platform licenses, uncertain ability to claim copyright in largely AI-generated uploads, third-party likeness/trademark/privacy exposure, and reduced licensing demand from substitutes. Training-data claims remain possible but costly and difficult to prove at an individual-image level.

### Cited Findings
- Copyright Office guidance calls for disclosure of AI-generated material in registration applications and requires applicants to identify human-authored contributions; registering an image without disclosing non-human material can jeopardize the application. [USCO registration guidance, 88 Fed. Reg. 16,190 (2023)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-03-16/pdf/2023-05321.pdf)
- The USCO recommends Congress consider a federal digital-replica law because existing state laws are uneven; its report describes risks from realistic unauthorized replicas of a person’s voice or likeness. This recommendation is not itself a federal statute. [USCO Part 1: Digital Replicas (2024)](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-1-Digital-Replicas-Report.pdf)
- EU AI Act Article 50 requires disclosure in specified cases of deepfake image/audio/video content; the obligation concerns providers/deployers and has exceptions, and is not a blanket watermark or copyright rule for all synthetic stock images. [AI Act, art. 50](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)
- US Copyright Office policy and *Thaler* establish that purely machine-generated output lacks copyright protection, while human selection, editing, or arrangement may remain protectable where independently creative. [USCO Part 2 report](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf); [*Thaler*](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/docs/Thaler-v-Perlmutter.pdf)
- Shutterstock’s stated policy bars contributor uploads of AI-generated assets, illustrating that platform eligibility and contract risk may be stricter than the law’s minimum requirements. [Shutterstock policy](https://www.shutterstock.com/contributorsupport/en/articles/10594621-AI-Generated-Content-Policy)
- *Andersen* and *Getty* plead or examine potential reproduction, removal of copyright-management information, output similarity, trademark, and distribution issues—distinct causes of action with different proof requirements. [Andersen docket](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/66732129/andersen-v-stability-ai-ltd/); [Getty docket](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/66725573/getty-images-us-inc-v-stability-ai-inc/)

### Inferences
- Strong provenance records (original files, dated edits, model/tool disclosure, releases, licenses, and platform-contract versions) improve a creator’s ability to establish authorship, permission, and the scope of rights granted.
- A generated image can pose rights risks even when its maker lacks copyright ownership: recognizable real people, protected logos, copied source expression, or misleading endorsements may implicate other legal regimes.
- Lost sales or stylistic similarity alone does not establish copyright infringement; a viable claim normally requires ownership of protected expression and actionable copying under applicable law.

### Gaps
- Rights-of-publicity, privacy, trademark, and personality rights vary substantially across US states and EU member states; this note does not map those jurisdiction-specific rules.
- No reliable public evidence here quantifies the causal effect of AI stock-image supply on individual contributors’ earnings or establishes a common industry-wide training royalty rate.
